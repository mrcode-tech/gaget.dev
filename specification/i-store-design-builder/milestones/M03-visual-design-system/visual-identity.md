# M03 — Visual Identity

## Builder identity

**Professional with creative character.** The builder should feel like a carefully crafted creative workspace: clear, composed, focused, confident, distinctive, and approachable to non-designers. Its personality should come from deliberate typography, proportion, spacing, hierarchy, restrained color, purposeful interaction, and quality details—not decorative effects.

## Principles

1. **Distinctive, not derivative:** learn from professional creative tools without copying their recognizable interface or branding.
2. **Canvas-first:** the user's website composition is the primary visual subject; controls support it.
3. **Quiet confidence:** use clear structure and restrained emphasis rather than constant decoration.
4. **Precision with warmth:** controls are predictable; language and guidance are welcoming.
5. **Coherent, not monotonous:** System A is consistent; System B samples may express different visual identities.
6. **Intentional hierarchy:** size, weight, contrast, spacing, and alignment communicate importance.
7. **Useful restraint:** borders, shadows, colors, animation, and icons need a functional or communicative reason.

## Avoid generic GPT/AI-product styling

Do not default to purple/blue gradients as an assumed AI identity, chat-first layouts for a visual-composition task, dashboards made entirely of repetitive rounded cards, glowing borders/glass effects without purpose, generic AI illustrations/robot/sparkle motifs, repetitive marketing-dashboard patterns, or excessive badges, pills, shadows, and borders. These are cautions rather than absolute bans: a technique may be used when justified by the design intent.

## Color, theme, and typography

Use a neutral foundation, restrained semantic colors, and a distinctive brand accent. Support light and dark themes from the beginning. Ensure accessible contrast and visible focus; never use color alone to communicate state.

Use a functional sans-serif foundation with a deliberate hierarchy for workspace headings, panel headings, sample names, body text, labels, metadata, and controls. Prioritize readability at actual workspace sizes; avoid expressive display typography for ordinary controls.

## Density and motion

Keep navigation and controls compact enough to preserve workspace utility; give canvases and sample previews generous room. Avoid turning every region into a bordered card. Use subtle purposeful motion for feedback, panel changes, insertion/reordering, preview transitions, and save/status feedback. Respect reduced-motion preferences and never delay work.

## Validation

Review onboarding, workspace, gallery, comparison, properties, three-canvas view, mission guide, progress, save/error feedback, and handoff. Validate light/dark themes, compact/spacious layouts, real content lengths, and keyboard/focus states.
